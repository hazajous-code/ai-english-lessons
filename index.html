Student handout (no answers; printable)

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Read a 500-word news text about AI and identify main ideas and key details
  2. Learn and use 12 topic words (e.g., estimate, fall behind, by default)
  3. Use reporting verbs (said, warned, claimed, admitted) and hedging language (suggest, may, seem) to separate facts from claims
  4. Discuss and negotiate a workplace policy on AI agents in a role-play

Before you read

04 · Reading

Have We Only Seen the Preview?

Adapted from AI Trend Search Vol.02 · about 520 words

1. A widening gap

On October 11, Andrej Karpathy, a co-founder of OpenAI who now works at Anthropic, wrote a post on X that got a lot of attention. His main point was simple: "The external world has seen the preview."

Karpathy described people's experience of AI as a funnel. About six billion people have barely used AI chatbots at all. One to two billion use free versions, mostly as "a better Google search" or for help with writing. Around 20 million professionals, mostly in software and math, use the most powerful models for serious work. Only about 5,000 people have access to the most advanced systems inside AI companies. These are his rough estimates, not survey results.

He also warned that "human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind." In other words, AI can now produce results faster than people can check them. Earlier that week, OpenAI had posted 722 math papers written with an unreleased model. The next day, it withdrew three of them because of a single sign error.

2. An office PC for your AI

Many new AI tools are no longer simple chatbots. They are agents: you give them a goal, and they carry out the steps themselves. Many agents work inside a virtual machine, a computer in the cloud that is kept separate from your own device. Inside it, an agent can open websites, fill in forms and keep working for hours. In June, Amazon Web Services launched a service that gives each user or task its own small virtual machine, which can pause and resume for up to eight hours.

Agents are now shopping for people. In September, Amazon blocked Meta's new AI agent, Muse, from its store. Amazon said Muse had not asked for permission, did not identify itself as an agent, and handled customers' login details. Meta did not respond to requests for comment.

3. AI in your pocket

AI is also moving onto chips. Phones can now run fairly large AI models without an internet connection: Qualcomm says its newest phone chip can run models with up to 30 billion parameters. A Canadian start-up called Taalas has gone further. It builds an AI model directly into a chip's circuits. Its chips are fast and use less power, but each one can run only one model. In August, the chipmaker AMD agreed to buy the company.

For ordinary users, the change is already visible. On iPhones, the Mail app now shows an AI-written summary of each email by default, so many people read the summary and never open the message.

4. Why it matters

Taken together, these stories suggest that more and more of what customers want now reaches companies through AI: an agent that shops for them, or a phone that summarizes their messages. The newsletter's editors argue that you can only understand customers as well as you understand the AI tools they use. That is an opinion, not a proven fact, but it is a question worth discussing.

05 · Comprehension

Check your understanding

  1. True or false? Karpathy's numbers come from a large survey.
  2. True or false? Karpathy thinks only a few thousand people have used the most advanced AI systems.
  3. True or false? OpenAI withdrew all 722 math papers.
  4. True or false? An agent in a virtual machine works directly on the user's own computer.
  5. Give two reasons why Amazon blocked Muse.
  6. What is one advantage and one limitation of Taalas's chips?
  7. What do many iPhone users read instead of opening an email?
  8. Is the main idea in the last paragraph a fact or an opinion? How do you know?
Answer key
  1. False — they are his rough estimates, not survey results.
  2. True — about 5,000 people.
  3. False — it withdrew three.
  4. False — it works on a separate computer in the cloud.
  5. Any two: it hadn't asked for permission; it didn't identify itself as an agent; it handled customers' login details.
  6. Advantage: fast / use less power. Limitation: each chip can run only one model.
  7. An AI-written summary that the Mail app shows by default.
  8. An opinion — the text says the editors "argue" it and calls it "an opinion, not a proven fact."

06 · Language focus

Reporting verbs and hedging: separating facts from claims

News writers use reporting verbs to show who said something and how they said it. The verb tells the reader the speaker's attitude: said is neutral, warned signals a risk, claimed suggests the writer hasn't checked it, admitted means the speaker accepted something negative.

Writers use hedging to avoid stating an opinion or prediction as a fact: suggest, may/might, seem, appear to, it is unclear whether.

From the text

  • Karpathy described people's experience of AI as a funnel.
  • He also warned that human review is starting to fall behind.
  • Amazon said Muse had not asked for permission. (reported speech: past → past perfect)
  • These stories suggest that more of what customers want reaches companies through AI.
  • The editors argue that you can only understand customers as well as you understand their AI tools.

Task 1

Choose the best reporting verb: warned, claimed, admitted, denied, announced, explained.

  1. The start-up ______ that its chip is ten times faster, but no one has tested it independently.
  2. The CEO ______ that the company had made a mistake and apologized.
  3. Experts ______ that small websites could become targets for cheap attacks.
  4. AMD ______ in August that it would buy Taalas.
  5. The spokesperson ______ that the company had collected any passwords.
  6. The teacher ______ how a virtual machine works, using a simple example.
Answer key
  1. claimed
  2. admitted
  3. warned
  4. announced
  5. denied
  6. explained

Task 2

Make these statements more cautious (hedge them). Use the word in brackets.

  1. AI agents will replace online shopping. (may)
  2. Customers trust email summaries more than the emails themselves. (seem)
  3. This survey proves that marketers are falling behind. (suggest)
Answer key

Suggested answers

  1. AI agents may change / may replace some online shopping.
  2. Customers seem to trust email summaries more than the emails themselves.
  3. This survey suggests that marketers may be falling behind.

07 · Discussion

Talk about it

Choose three questions and discuss them in small groups.

  1. Where are you on Karpathy's "funnel"? Where are most people in your workplace?
  2. Would you let an AI agent shop or book things for you? What would you not let it do?
  3. Was Amazon right to block Meta's agent? Should stores welcome AI agents or keep them out?
  4. Do you read AI summaries instead of full emails or articles? What might you miss?
  5. Karpathy works at an AI company. Does that change how you read his claims? Why?
  6. Do you agree with the editors' opinion in the last paragraph? Why or why not?

08 · Role-play

Meeting: Should our online store let AI agents in?

You work for a mid-sized online electronics store. Customers' AI agents have started visiting the site to compare prices and buy products. The company must decide on a policy this week.

A — E-commerce manager You want to allow agents. They bring new customers and sales. Suggest rules that make it safe.

B — Head of security / customer service You want to block agents for now. You worry about fraud, customer passwords and complaints when an agent makes a mistake.

C — Director (chairs the meeting) Listen to both sides, ask questions, and make a final decision. You may choose a middle option (e.g., allow browsing but require customer approval for payment).

Useful language

In my view, we should… · The main risk is that… · What if we…? · I see your point, but… · Could we agree to…? · So, to sum up, we've decided to…

09 · Writing

Your turn to write

Write a short email (120–150 words) to your manager. Recommend one rule your company should have about AI agents (for example, on your website or for staff using AI tools). Give two reasons and use at least two reporting verbs or hedging expressions from today's lesson.

10 · Teacher's notes

Tips for this lesson

  • Focus point: the text distinguishes facts from claims and estimates. Ask students to find each place where the writer signals this ("rough estimates", "says", "argue", "an opinion, not a proven fact").
  • Pronunciation: estimate (noun /ˈestɪmət/ vs verb /ˈestɪmeɪt/), circuit /ˈsɜːkɪt/, withdraw /wɪðˈdrɔː/.
  • Common error: reported speech after a past reporting verb — "Amazon said Muse had not asked", not "has not asked" (both may be acceptable when the fact is still true; discuss).
  • Lower-level groups: pre-teach chip, cloud and parameter with simple pictures or gestures, and read Part 1 together before pair reading.
  • Higher-level groups: ask students to find the original newsletter (link above) and compare how the business version frames the same facts.
